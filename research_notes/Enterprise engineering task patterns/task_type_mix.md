# Enterprise engineering task-type mix: what software engineers in established companies actually spend time on

Scope note for the writer: almost no public source measures the *distribution* of enterprise engineering time across all the task types asked about (bugs, incidents, features, upgrades, security, compliance, tech debt, review, docs, support) in one instrument. The picture below is assembled from (a) self-reported time surveys, mostly vendor-sponsored, (b) two Microsoft-internal studies, (c) one large Jira-archive study whose trackers are public-facing (a mix of company-run and foundation-run instances), and (d) vendor documentation for tracker taxonomies. Sources before 2022 are flagged as such. Where a figure comes from a search-engine summary of a paper rather than the paper text, it is marked "(via search summary)".

## Key question 1: What share of engineering time goes to maintenance, bugs and incidents versus new features?

### Takeaway
Every survey that asks finds that "new feature" coding is a minority of the week, typically 10–35% depending on how narrowly it is defined, while maintenance in the broad sense (debugging, fixing, upgrading, patching, tech debt, toil) accounts for roughly 30–50% and the remainder goes to coordination, meetings, reviews and finding information. No 2022–2026 source gives a clean enterprise-only split by task type; the most recent direct measurements are a 2024 Microsoft-internal time-allocation study and 2025 vendor surveys.

### Cited Findings

Direct time-allocation measurements (most useful, but each has caveats):
- Microsoft "Time Warp" study (484 individual-contributor engineers at Microsoft in the US and India, surveyed June–July 2024, self-reported hours over the last five workdays): actual week ≈12% communication & meetings, ≈11% coding, ≈9% debugging, ≈6% architecting/designing, ≈5% PR/code review; least time went to learning, mentoring/onboarding, tech talks and setting up monitoring/dashboards. Ideal week: ≈20% coding, ≈15% architecting. Developers wanted less time on debugging, customer support tickets, and Security & Compliance, and more on reviews, documentation, mentoring and learning. Time on Security & Compliance beyond the ideal was negatively associated with satisfaction (coef −0.0090, p=0.001); time in the development environment (setup/tooling) beyond ideal was negatively associated with both productivity and satisfaction. — [arXiv 2502.15287](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15287v1)
- Chainguard "2026 Engineering Reality Report" (fielded August 2025; 600 software engineers + 600 technology leaders across US/UK/Germany/France; vendor-funded): engineers report spending only ~16% of their week writing code and building new features; 79% point to code maintenance as a major drain; 72% say demands on their time make it hard to find space for new features; 38% cite "frequent code maintenance (upgrades, breaking changes, patching, vulnerability resolution)" as a reason their developer experience is not highly positive, the same share cite tedious tasks; 66% frequently or very frequently encounter technical debt that hinders delivery; 70% use more than five tools weekly. — [Chainguard](https://www.chainguard.dev/2026-engineering-reality-report?gated=no)
- Meyer, Barr, Bird, Zimmermann, "Today was a Good Day: The Daily Life of Software Developers" (IEEE TSE 2019; 5,971 responses from Microsoft developers over four months): developers "actually spend little time on development"; good days are those spent mostly alone on development-related tasks; meetings and interruptions are only unproductive during development phases. The paper's background section cites earlier studies putting coding at 9%–61% of time depending on study, and a separate 20-developer field study putting coding-related activities at about one quarter of work time (via search summary; the paper PDF could not be parsed). — [DX summary](https://getdx.com/research/daily-life-of-software-developers/); [UCL open-access PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10090507/1/Barr_Today%20was%20a%20Good%20Day.%20The%20Daily%20Life%20of%20Software%20Developers_AAM2.pdf)
- Gartner is cited second-hand as estimating developers spend 20–30% of their time writing code (not verified against a Gartner document). — [ITPro](https://www.itpro.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/most-developers-will-soon-use-an-ai-pair-programmer-but-the-benefits-arent-black-and-white)

Maintenance-share surveys (older, but the most-quoted numbers):
- Stripe "The Developer Coefficient" (2018, survey of developers and C-level executives; self-estimated hours): developers spend more than 17 hours per week on maintenance issues such as debugging, refactoring and modifying bad code, split as 13.5 h on technical debt and 3.8 h on "bad code"; Stripe extrapolated this to ~$85B/year globally. Commentators noted the figures are self-estimates. (2018 — old.) — [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/?p=541182); [I Programmer](https://www.i-programmer.info/news/99-professional/12118-old-and-bad-code-waste-billions.html)
- Sonar developer survey (fielded Nov–Dec 2018, published March 2019, ~300 professional developers): code maintenance takes ~30% of the average developer's week (~12 h); 70% of respondents spend 11%–50% of their time on maintenance (4.4–20 h/week); larger teams report a larger maintenance share (authors suspect older, larger codebases); open source packages account for ~25% of the maintenance workload on average, and nearly 10% of respondents say 76–100% of their maintenance relates to open source packages. (2018/2019 — old.) — [Sonar](https://www.sonarsource.com/blog/developers-spend-30-of-their-time-on-code-maintenance-our-latest-survey-results-part-3/)
- Tidelift open source maintainer survey (mid-2024; open source maintainers, not enterprise developers — weaker evidence for this question): ~50% of time on day-to-day maintenance (53% in 2021), 35% on building new features (25% in 2021), 11% on security work (4% in 2021). — [Tidelift via DEV](https://dev.to/tidelift/maintainers-are-spending-3x-more-time-on-security-than-they-did-a-few-years-ago-3g89)

Time lost / friction surveys (they measure waste and frustration, not allocation):
- Atlassian + DX "State of Developer Experience 2024" (1,250 engineering leaders surveyed by Wakefield in US/DE/FR/AU; 900 developers surveyed by DX globally): 69% of developers lose 8 or more hours per week to inefficiencies; ranked causes: technical debt (top), insufficient documentation, build processes, lack of time for deep work, lack of clear direction (per-cause percentages are only in a chart image); 62% of developers said AI tools had little or no impact on their productivity at the time. — [DX newsletter on the report](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/state-of-developer-experience-2024)
- Atlassian "State of Developer Experience 2025": top time-wasters are now finding information (services, docs, APIs), adapting new technology, and context switching between tools; technical debt fell out of the top 5; 99% of developers report time savings from AI tools and 68% say they save more than 10 hours per week (via search summary of the report page). — [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/teams/software-development/state-of-developer-experience-2025)
- Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2024: 62.4% selected "amount of technical debt" as their top frustration, followed by "complexity of tech stack for build" (32.9%) and "complexity of tech stack for deployment" (32.3%). — [Stack Overflow blog](https://stackoverflow.blog/2024/06/24/developers-want-more-more-more-the-2024-results-from-stack-overflow-s-annual-developer-survey/)
- A secondary analysis of the 2024 Stack Overflow data reports 61% of developers spend over 30 minutes a day searching for answers and 48.8% often answer questions they have already answered before (not verified against the official results). — [RDEL #54](https://rdel.substack.com/p/rdel-54-analyzing-the-2024-stack)

Operations / toil share:
- Google SRE book, "Eliminating Toil": toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical production work with no enduring value that scales with the service; Google's stated goal is to keep toil below 50% of each SRE's time; quarterly surveys found average time toiling is about 33%, with individual reports ranging from 0% to 80%; the top sources are interrupts (non-urgent service messages), on-call response, and releases/pushes. (Book published 2016 — old, but still the reference point for "toil budget".) — [Google SRE](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)
- PagerDuty "State of Digital Operations 2024" (enterprise and upper mid-market decision makers): a 16% increase in enterprise incidents year over year. Earlier PagerDuty platform data (2021): the median responder had 2 interruptions per month and the 90th-percentile responder had 19 non-working-hour interruptions a month; 54% of responders are interrupted outside normal working hours. — [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240207764183/en/PagerDuty-Study-Finds-16-Increase-in-Enterprise-Incidents-Amid-Race-to-AI-Adoption); [PagerDuty](https://www.pagerduty.com/resources/digital-transformation/webinar/impact-of-on-call-and-real-time-work/)

Security as a slice of the week:
- IDC InfoBrief "The Hidden Cost of DevSecOps" (Sept 2024, sponsored by JFrog): half of respondents estimate ~19% of their weekly hours go to security-related tasks, often outside normal hours; developers spend 3.5 h/week on average manually reviewing security-scan findings because of false positives and duplicates; estimated ~$28K per developer per year on identifying, evaluating and addressing security concerns. — [JFrog/IDC press release](https://s201.q4cdn.com/814780939/files/doc_news/2024/10/final_idc_devsecurity-pr_oct2024.pdf)
- Checkmarx-commissioned surveys report much higher shares: 49% of a developer's week on security-related issues (2,350 respondents), and 72% of developers saying they spend more than 17 hours per week on security tasks (1,500 respondents). These are vendor surveys and not comparable to the IDC figure. — [ITPro](https://www.itpro.com/software/development/software-developers-are-spending-more-time-every-week-fixing-security-issues-and-its-costing-companies-a-fortune); [ITPro](https://www.itpro.com/security/developers-spend-17-hours-security-dont-consider-it-a-top-priority)

How work is organised (allocation accounting):
- Jellyfish's allocation benchmarking groups engineering investment into innovation, support, and "keeping the lights on" (KTLO), measured in FTEs, and compares opt-in customers against an anonymised pool; no public KTLO percentage benchmark was found. — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/jellyfish-now-lets-software-engineering-teams-benchmark-performance-against-peers); [Jellyfish](https://jellyfish.co/blog/work-allocations-model-for-efficient-engineering-teams/)

### Inferences
- Across the Microsoft 2024 study (coding ≈11%, debugging ≈9%, reviews ≈5%) and the Chainguard 2025 survey (≈16% on code and new features), "writing new product code" is plausibly 10–20% of an enterprise engineer's week; the rest is split between fixing/maintaining and coordination. This is consistent with the Gartner 20–30% coding estimate if coding includes fix and upgrade work.
- Treating "maintenance" broadly (debugging + bug fixing + upgrades + patching + tech debt + toil), the 2018 Stripe (~40% of a 40-hour week) and Sonar (~30%) figures and the 2024 Tidelift maintainer figure (50%) bracket a 30–50% band. Nothing since 2022 contradicts that band for enterprises, but nothing since 2022 measures it directly either.
- Security and compliance is the fastest-growing slice in the data that exists: Tidelift's 4% → 11% (2021 → 2024, OSS) and IDC's 19% (2024, enterprise developers) are both well above the few hours per month WhiteSource reported around 2018 (see question 4).
- The shift in Atlassian's "top time waster" from technical debt (2024) to finding information (2025) suggests that in enterprises the cost is increasingly in locating context (services, docs, APIs, owners) rather than in the code change itself.

### Gaps
- No DORA 2023–2025 report content was retrieved in this research; DORA measures delivery performance (lead time, deployment frequency, change failure rate, recovery) and does not publish a time-by-task-type distribution, so it cannot answer this question directly.
- JetBrains State of Developer Ecosystem and GitHub Octoverse were checked; neither publishes a time allocation by task type (Octoverse 2024 reports that 82% of contributions go to private repositories but does not split maintenance from new work).
- McKinsey's 2023 "Yes, you can measure software developer productivity" is cited second-hand for a recommendation that developers spend ≥70% of time on "inner loop" work; the primary document and any measured percentage were not retrieved.
- No source gives separate shares for incidents/hotfixes vs. planned bug fixes vs. feature work for enterprise teams; the Microsoft study's closest category is "customer support tickets" (no exact percentage reported in the text).

## Key question 2: Which issue types and categories do enterprise trackers use, and what is known about their relative volumes?

### Takeaway
Default taxonomies are small and similar across vendors (Jira: Epic/Story/Task/Bug/Subtask; Jira Service Management: Incident/Problem/Change/Service request/IT help/Support/New feature; Azure DevOps: Epic/Feature/User Story or PBI or Requirement/Task/Bug plus Issue/Impediment/Change Request/Risk/Review depending on process), but real instances customise heavily (a median of 14 issue types per Jira instance). In the only large archival study found (2.7M issues, 16 public Jira instances including Red Hat, MongoDB, JFrog, Qt, Spring, Apache), bug reports are ~50% of issues, requirements-type issues (Improvement, New Feature, Story) ~30%, and Tasks/Sub-tasks ~20%. These trackers are public-facing, so they over-represent bugs relative to an internal enterprise sprint board.

### Cited Findings

Vendor default taxonomies:
- Jira default issue types. Software projects: Bug ("a problem which impairs or prevents the functions of a product"), Story ("the smallest unit of work that needs to be done"), Task ("work that needs to be done"), Subtask, and Epic ("a big user story that needs to be broken down"). Jira Service Management projects: Change ("requesting a change in the current IT profile"), IT help, Incident ("reporting an incident or IT service outage"), New feature, Problem ("investigating and reporting the root cause of multiple incidents"), Service request, Service request with approval, Support. Business (Work Management) projects: Task and Subtask only. — [Atlassian support](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-cloud-administration/docs/what-are-issue-types/)
- Azure DevOps (Azure Boards) default processes. Basic: Epic, Issue, Task (states To Do/Doing/Done). Agile: Epic, Feature, User Story, Task, Bug (optional on backlog), Issue (states New/Active/Resolved/Closed/Removed). Scrum: Epic, Feature, Product Backlog Item, Task, Bug, Impediment (states New/Approved/Committed/Done/Removed). CMMI: Epic, Feature, Requirement, Task, Bug, Change Request, Issue, Risk, Review (states Proposed/Active/Resolved/Closed). Microsoft recommends CMMI when "you work in a regulated environment that requires formal change control, an auditable record of decisions, and risk and review tracking." Hidden types shared by all non-Basic processes: Test Plan, Test Suite, Test Case, Shared Steps, Shared Parameters, Feedback Request/Response, Code Review Request/Response. — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/boards/work-items/guidance/choose-process)
- ServiceNow ITSM: the Benchmark KPIs application compares a customer's instance against anonymised global and industry benchmarks on KPIs including the share of resolved incidents linked to a problem, the share of emergency changes, the share of failed changes, average time to close a change, and incidents created per user; data refreshes monthly. ServiceNow ships normal, standard and emergency change models. — [ServiceNow docs](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/it-service-management/benchmarks/c_BenchKPIConfig-cf.html); [ServiceNow community](https://www.servicenow.com/community/itsm-articles/servicenow-itsm-explained-with-real-business-examples-beyond/ta-p/3466352)

Measured volumes:
- Montgomery, "Issue Tracking Ecosystems: Context and Best Practices" (PhD thesis, arXiv July 2025). Dataset: 16 public Jira repositories (Apache, Hyperledger, IntelDAOS, JFrog, Jira, JiraEcosystem, MariaDB, Mindville, Mojang, MongoDB, Qt, RedHat, Sakai, SecondLife, Sonatype, Spring) with 2,686,282 issues, 1,822 projects and 352 distinct issue types in use; median 14 issue types per repository. Across the 13 repositories with comments (1,327,703 issues): Maintenance 52% (Bug Report 684,740 ≈ 51.6%), Requirements 29% (Improvement Suggestion 291,837 ≈ 22.0%, New Feature 49,793 ≈ 3.8%, Story 20,441 ≈ 1.5%, Feature Request 20,175 ≈ 1.5%), Development 19% (Task 139,003 ≈ 10.5%, Sub-Task 93,763 ≈ 7.1%), Epic 4,290 ≈ 0.3%. Per-project medians over 1,477 projects: Maintenance 50% (Bug Report 48%), Requirements 34% (in a quarter of projects 47–81%), Development 19% (Task 12%, Sub-Task 5%), Epic 2%; in 25% of projects at least 40% of issues are User Stories ("heavily Story-driven" projects). 186 distinct issue-type sets are used; the most common set (139 projects, 9.4%) is New Feature + Improvement + Task + Bug; 86 projects (5.8%) use Bug only; the top 30 type sets cover 75% of projects. About 12 fields are filled on average at issue creation, often with null defaults; Summary/Description edits are rare after creation (median 0% of an issue's evolutions). — [arXiv 2507.06704](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06704)
- Bug-vs-non-bug share in classification datasets: Jira projects 61% bug-related vs 39% non-bug; Bugzilla 78% vs 22% (via search summary). — [arXiv 2503.00660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.00660)
- Five open-source Jira projects (IMEKO TC4, 2014 — old): Bug 46–49%, Improvement ~33%, New Feature 8–12%, Task 4–11%; the authors note Task is loosely defined (via search summary). — [IMEKO 2014](https://imeko.org/publications/tc4-2014/IMEKO-TC4-2014-417.pdf)
- Process-mining study of industrial TFS/Azure DevOps projects (Coremans et al., 2023): developers mostly used Epic, Story, Bug and Sub-task; the Task type was not used in the projects studied (via search summary). — [Coremans 2023](https://jacobkrueger.github.io/assets/papers/Coremans2023ProcessMiningTFS.pdf)

Organisation of the work:
- Azure DevOps work items carry Area (product/team), Iteration (sprint), State and Reason; Tasks carry Original Estimate, Remaining Work, Completed Work and Activity (for capacity planning by activity type); User Stories carry Story Points and Stack Rank; Priority is a 1–3 scale ("1: product can't ship without the feature"). — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/boards/work-items/guidance/agile-process-workflow)

### Inferences
- Enterprise instances combine two taxonomies: a software-delivery one (Epic/Story/Task/Bug in Jira Software or Azure Boards) and an ITIL one (Incident/Problem/Change/Service request in Jira Service Management or ServiceNow). Engineering teams typically receive the ITIL-side work as linked or re-typed tickets, so an engineer's inbound queue mixes both.
- The ~50% bug share in public Jira instances is an upper bound for internal enterprise boards, where Stories and Tasks dominate sprint planning and bugs from users are filtered through support; the "heavily Story-driven" quartile (≥40% Stories) in the same dataset is probably closer to a product team's internal board.
- Because the median instance uses 14 issue types and only ~9% of projects share the most common type set, any automation that classifies work by issue type must be configured per project rather than relying on defaults.

### Gaps
- No published incident-to-problem-to-change volume ratio for enterprise ITSM was found; ServiceNow's benchmarks are visible only inside customer instances.
- No source gives the relative volume of Jira Service Management request types (Incident vs Service request vs Change) across enterprises.
- Official ServiceNow documentation defining Incident/Problem/Change was not retrieved (results were community posts and third-party guides); the definitions used above come from Atlassian's equivalent types and ServiceNow community material.

## Key question 3: What does a typical enterprise bug or incident ticket contain, and what is typically missing?

### Takeaway
Developers consistently rank steps to reproduce, stack traces and test cases as the most useful ticket content, and these are exactly the items reporters find hardest to supply; where measured, the great majority of bug reports lack at least one reproduction step. Incident studies at Microsoft show most high-severity incidents are mitigated without a code fix, with documentation and monitoring gaps delaying a fifth or more of them. Only Title is mandatory in Azure DevOps; logs, records and environment details arrive as attachments and links if at all.

### Cited Findings
- Bettenburg, Just, Schröter, Weiss, Premraj, Zimmermann, "What Makes a Good Bug Report?" (FSE 2008; 466 survey responses from developers and users of Apache, Eclipse and Mozilla): the items most helpful to developers are steps to reproduce, stack traces and test cases; these are also the items users find hardest to provide (an "information mismatch"); inaccurate steps and incomplete information are the largest hurdles; developers were largely indifferent to duplicates. A quality predictor (CUEZILLA) trained on 289 rated reports predicted quality accurately for 31–48% of reports. (2008 — old; open source projects.) — [Never Work in Theory summary](https://neverworkintheory.org/2011/08/30/what-makes-a-good-bug-report.html); [Saarland TR](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/details/bettenburg-tr-2007)
- Zimmermann et al., IEEE TSE 2010 (466 responses): confirms the mismatch between what developers need (steps to reproduce, stack traces, test cases) and what reporters supply (2010 — old). — [Zimmermann TSE 2010](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/zimmermann-tse-2010.pdf)
- Chaparro et al. (2019), Euler: automatically detects missing or low-quality steps to reproduce; identified 98% of existing steps and 58% of missing ones, with 73% of quality annotations judged correct. — [arXiv 1906.07107](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.07107)
- Empirical study of Android bug report reproduction (2023): 92% of reports have at least one missing reproduction step, so developers must infer the missing operations. (Open source / mobile — weaker for enterprise.) — [arXiv 2301.01235](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.01235)
- Empirical Software Engineering (2020) study of bug report elements and resolution time: when a stack trace is missing, developers typically ask the reporter for it and close the report if nothing arrives within a month (via search summary). — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3280997/view)
- Microsoft Teams production incidents, "How to Fight Production Incidents?" (SoCC 2022; 152 high-severity incidents and their postmortems): about 40% of incidents were caused by code or configuration bugs, but nearly 80% were mitigated without a code or configuration fix; in 21% of incidents where manual effort delayed mitigation, the delay pointed to gaps in documentation and training. — [SoCC 2022 paper](https://www.aminer.org/pub/637db89665b12e000ca13df2/how-to-fight-production-incidents-an-empirical-study-on-a-large-scale); [slides](https://asatarin.github.io/assets/talks/2023-01-how-to-fight-production-incidents.pdf)
- Microsoft monitoring-gap study ("Detection Is Better Than Cure"): in 30% of incidents studied, existing monitors had incorrect logic or lacked appropriate signals/telemetry. AutoTSG (2022) analysed 4,000+ troubleshooting guides mapped to thousands of incidents and found TSGs significantly reduce mitigation effort. — [Ganatra et al.](https://jonathanmace.github.io/papers/ganatra2023detection.pdf); [AutoTSG](https://export.arxiv.org/abs/2205.13457?context=cs)
- Azure DevOps ticket anatomy (Agile process): Title is the only universally required field; Description ("who the feature is for, what users want to accomplish, and why"), Acceptance Criteria ("criteria to be met before the bug or user story can be closed"), Priority 1–3, Risk, Value Area, Story Points; Attachments tab for "documents, images, logs, or email threads"; Links tab for parent/child items, changesets, source files, test results; Integrated in Build ("the product build number that incorporates the code or fixes a bug"); Discussion/History for comments. — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/boards/work-items/guidance/agile-process-workflow)
- Jira archive study: ~12 fields are filled on average at issue creation, often with null defaults, and Summary/Description are rarely edited after creation (median 0% of evolutions; upper quartile 6%). — [arXiv 2507.06704](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06704)
- Atlassian 2024: insufficient documentation is the second-ranked cause of lost developer time; Atlassian 2025: finding information about services, docs and APIs is the top time-waster. — [DX summary of 2024](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/state-of-developer-experience-2024); [Atlassian 2025](https://www.atlassian.com/teams/software-development/state-of-developer-experience-2025)

### Inferences
- The content a ticket reliably carries is: title, free-text description, priority/severity, component/area, assignee, sprint, and whatever the reporter chose to attach. What the engineer must usually go and find: exact reproduction steps, the stack trace or log excerpt, the affected record identifiers and environment/build, and (for incidents) which monitor fired and what the runbook says. The Microsoft data (80% of severe incidents mitigated without a code fix; 21% delayed by documentation gaps) implies the investigative part of an incident ticket dominates the coding part.
- The 2024 → 2025 shift toward "finding information" as the main time sink is consistent with tickets being thin: the specification lives in systems (logs, databases, dashboards, chat) the ticket only points to.

### Gaps
- No 2022–2026 study of *enterprise-internal* bug or incident ticket content was found; the quantitative ticket-quality literature is on open source trackers (Apache, Eclipse, Mozilla, Android apps). The Microsoft incident papers are enterprise-grade but cover cloud-service incidents, not line-of-business bug tickets.
- The Azure DevOps Bug-specific fields (Repro Steps, System Info, Severity, Found In Build) were not confirmed from the page fetched; the page documented User Story and Task fields in detail and only general bug guidance.
- No data on how often enterprise tickets include record IDs, database rows or environment details.

## Key question 4: Which task types are recurring and mechanical, and how large a share are they?

### Takeaway
Dependency/framework upgrades, CVE patching, vulnerability-scan triage, flaky-test repair, certificate/config changes and release pushes are the recurring mechanical categories; where measured, security work alone is ~11–19% of time in 2024 surveys, scan triage ~3.5 h/week, flaky tests ≥2.5% of developer time (or ~8% of QA time), and Java major-version upgrades historically ~50 developer-days per application. Enterprises report automating these categories at roughly two-thirds adoption (Chainguard 2025), and vendors claim order-of-magnitude reductions from AI tooling.

### Cited Findings
- Chainguard 2025 survey: 38% of engineers cite frequent code maintenance (upgrades, breaking changes, patching, vulnerability resolution) as a reason their DevEx is not highly positive; share of organisations that "mostly or fully automate" each activity: testing/monitoring/QA 68%, security patching and vulnerability remediation 67%, incident response and troubleshooting 66%, code review 65%, maintaining code (upgrades, breaking changes, base images) 64%, writing code/docs/features 63%. — [Chainguard](https://www.chainguard.dev/2026-engineering-reality-report?gated=no)
- IDC/JFrog 2024: ~19% of weekly hours on security tasks; 3.5 h/week reviewing scan findings because of false positives and duplicates; 50% of developers' security time spent understanding secrets-scanning results, code changes and secrets management. — [JFrog/IDC](https://s201.q4cdn.com/814780939/files/doc_news/2024/10/final_idc_devsecurity-pr_oct2024.pdf)
- Tidelift 2024 (OSS maintainers): security work 11% of time, up from 4% in 2021. — [Tidelift](https://dev.to/tidelift/maintainers-are-spending-3x-more-time-on-security-than-they-did-a-few-years-ago-3g89)
- WhiteSource/Mend survey (~650 developers, US and Western Europe, c. 2018 — old): ~15 hours per month dealing with open source security vulnerabilities, of which only ~25% (~3.8 h) is actual remediation; more experienced developers tend to be assigned remediation. — [Mend](https://www.mend.io/?p=6001)
- Veracode State of Software Security vol. 11, open source edition (Cyentia analysis): 79% of third-party libraries are never updated by developers once integrated into a codebase. (c. 2021 — older.) — [Cyentia](https://www.cyentia.com/publication/state-of-software-security-vol-11-open-source-edition/)
- GitHub Octoverse 2024: developers are merging an increasing number of Dependabot pull requests and the gap between Dependabot PRs opened and merged has narrowed each year (no absolute figures); Copilot Autofix cut median time to fix a PR-time code-scanning alert from 1.5 hours to 28 minutes (XSS from ~3 h to 22 min; SQL injection from 3.7 h to 18 min); secret scanning detected 39M+ leaked secrets in 2024; 82%+ of contributions go to private repositories. — [GitHub Octoverse 2024](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-2024/)
- Dependabot security PRs in 2,904 JavaScript projects (MSR 2021): 65.42% of security-related PRs were merged, often within a day; 34.58% were not merged. A separate Dependabot study found dependency vulnerabilities stay hidden for 512 days on average and patches are disclosed after 362 days (via search summary). An ecosyste.ms index reports a 12% merge rate across the Dependabot PRs it tracks (different scope). — [MSR 2021](https://realiselab.github.io/publications/On-the-Use-of-Dependabot-Security-Pull-Requests.pdf); [EMSE 2024 (Luxembourg)](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/62972/1/s10664-024-10523-y%20%281%29.pdf); [ecosyste.ms](https://dependabot.ecosyste.ms/about)
- Java version/framework upgrades: Amazon states the average upgrade of an application to Java 17 took ~50 developer-days before Amazon Q code transformation and "a few hours" after, estimating 4,500 developer-years saved (vendor claim from the CEO). An AWS-documented customer pilot on 4 applications reported a 36% acceleration / 42% time saving, ~24 hours saved per application; the tool targets Spring Boot up to 3.2 and the 30 most common Java libraries, with lower performance on uncommon/internal libraries. — [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/amazons-ai-assistant-saves-4500-years-of-development-time-ceo-andy-jassy-says/); [AWS DevOps blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/accelerate-application-upgrades-with-amazon-q-developer-agent-for-code-transformation/)
- Flaky tests: ICST 2024 industrial case study (one team of ~30 developers): time dealing with flaky tests is at least 2.5% of productive developer time (1.1% investigating potentially flaky failures, 1.3% repairing flaky tests, 0.1% building monitoring tools), described as a lower bound. Bitrise survey (fielded mid/late 2023; 1,615 respondents, 70 countries): 7.8% of QA time spent fixing flaky tests (self-reported). — [ICST 2024](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icst-2024/icst-2024-industry/1/Cost-of-Flaky-Tests-in-CI-An-Industrial-Case-Study); [TUM](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1730194/1730194.pdf); [Panto summary of Bitrise](https://www.getpanto.ai/blog/flaky-test-statistics)
- Google SRE toil: interrupts, on-call and releases/pushes are the three largest toil sources; ~33% of SRE time on average. — [Google SRE](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)
- Code review: a 2024–2025 survey of 100 developers at five companies found a median of ~3 hours per week on code review (authors say not representative), compared with a median of ~4 hours in the 2019 Stack Overflow survey. — [arXiv 2508.06879](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06879v4)

### Inferences
- Summing the measured mechanical slices for a typical enterprise developer in 2024–2025 (security/patching ~11–19%, flaky tests ~2–3%, code review ~5–8%, plus upgrade and release work that is not separately measured) gives a recurring-mechanical band of roughly a quarter to a third of the week, which sits inside the 30–50% "maintenance" band from question 1. This is an inference across non-comparable surveys, not a measured number.
- The two-thirds automation adoption rates (Chainguard) and the GitHub/AWS vendor numbers indicate that patching, dependency bumps and version migrations are the task types enterprises are most actively trying to hand to tooling, which is consistent with them being specifiable from machine-readable inputs (CVE id, package, target version) rather than from a human-written ticket.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies certificate rotation, configuration changes or feature-flag changes as a share of engineering time.
- No recent (2022+) survey of enterprise developers measures hours on dependency updates specifically; the WhiteSource figure is c. 2018.
- Octoverse 2024 gives no percentages for the share of pull requests that are automated or Dependabot-generated.

## Key question 5: How does the mix differ in heavily regulated industries (finance, airlines, telecom, healthcare)?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin. What exists shows regulated firms carry formal change-control overhead (CAB approval of >90% of major changes, auditable process templates, large audit-evidence workloads) and that change management failures are the leading cause of incidents reported to the UK FCA; no source quantifies how the engineering time mix itself differs by industry.

### Cited Findings
- UK Financial Conduct Authority, "Implementing Technology Change" (published 5 February 2021; analysis of over 1 million production changes at regulated firms): change management failures are the most common cause of incidents reported to the FCA; Change Advisory Boards approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, and in some firms the CAB did not reject a single change during 2019; firms deploying smaller, more frequent releases had higher change success rates; firms using agile delivery methodologies were less likely to experience change incidents. (2021 — slightly outside the preferred window.) — [Kosli summary of the FCA report](https://www.kosli.com/blog/what-the-fca-found-when-analysing-1-million-production-changes/)
- Microsoft recommends the CMMI process (adds Requirement, Change Request, Risk and Review work item types) for teams "in a regulated environment that requires formal change control, an auditable record of decisions, and risk and review tracking". — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/devops/boards/work-items/guidance/choose-process)
- Vanson Bourne study of 200 US financial-services IT security professionals (2021): 97% of institutions experience challenges when working on audits; financial organisations spend an average of 71 working days each quarter responding to audit evidence requests. — [Vumetric summary](https://cyber.vumetric.com/security-news/2021/12/09/how-can-organizations-ease-audit-overload)
- SolarWinds (ITSM for financial services): developers deploying and approving their own changes is a common audit finding; regulators want audit trails on changes. — [SolarWinds](https://www.solarwinds.com/blog/itsm-for-financial-services-change-management-audit-trails-and-what-regulators-actually-want)
- Microsoft "Time Warp" (2024): Security & Compliance was among the activities developers most wanted to spend less time on, and excess time on it was significantly negatively associated with satisfaction (coef −0.0090, p=0.001). — [arXiv 2502.15287](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15287v1)
- AscentAI 2026 RegTech benchmark (compliance teams, not engineers): 57% cite slow, manual processes as their greatest regulatory challenge; monthly hours on regulatory change management: 27% under 10 h, 25% 10–20 h, 22% 21–30 h. — [Ascent](https://www.ascentregtech.com/blog/manual-compliance-is-costing-your-team-more-than-you-think/)

### Inferences
- In regulated industries, the same engineering task (a dependency bump, a hotfix) carries additional ticket artefacts: a Change Request/Change record with approval, risk classification (normal/standard/emergency), and evidence for audit. This increases the share of time spent on specification and documentation per change rather than changing the task mix itself; the FCA finding that >90% of major changes are approved suggests the CAB step is largely administrative overhead.
- The FCA's finding that change failures are the top incident cause implies that in finance a larger share of incident tickets trace back to a recent change record, which is useful context for incident tickets (the change id is a key missing-information item).

### Gaps
- No survey or dataset breaks down engineering time by task type per industry; DORA publishes industry cuts of delivery metrics but not time allocation, and no DORA industry data was retrieved here.
- Nothing specific was found for airlines, telecom or healthcare engineering time mixes.
- The FCA report's own figures for emergency-change share, change failure rate and incident attribution percentages were not retrieved (only the Kosli summary was read).
